Hyperfine EIT with a 6.834 GHz EOM

The second route to Λ-EIT in ⁸⁷Rb D1. B1

Mod B at a glance

What Mod B is for in this project, and what it is not for.

Idea. Mod A builds its Λ inside one hyperfine level (Zeeman sublevels, kHz apart), so two AOMs suffice. Mod B spans the two ground hyperfine levels, F=1 and F=2, 6.834 GHz apart. The paper notes that AOM double passing works up to ≈ 3.5 GHz and that, for higher modulation frequencies, “electro-optical modulators are the best choice”. Hyperfine EIT therefore needs either an EOM or two phase-locked lasers.

Role in the project. It is a complementary CW characterisation path on the same cell and laser. It gives a field-insensitive (clock-transition) EIT resonance to compare with Mod A’s Zeeman line, and a GHz-referenced measurement of OD, linewidth and decoherence. Objectives 1–2, including pulsed storage, stay on Mod A (see the decision record).

Shared with Mod A. DFB laser, isolator, SAS lock, cell, heater, TC300B, waveplates, PBSs, mounts, and the shield and solenoid (a gap in both cases).

B2

Physics of hyperfine Λ-EIT

Only what differs from Mod A. For the common three-level theory (dark state, susceptibility, slow light) see Mod A §3b–3d; for background see Lecture 5 and Lecture 7.

2a · Which levels, which field is which

The paper’s hyperfine example (Fig. 9a) uses |1⟩ = 5S₁/₂ F=2, |2⟩ = 5S₁/₂ F=1 and |3⟩ = 5P₁/₂ F′=1. The probe couples |1⟩→|3⟩ (F=2→F′=1) and the control couples |2⟩→|3⟩ (F=1→F′=1). In ⁸⁷Rb the F=2 ground level lies 6.834 GHz above F=1, so the F=2→F′ transition is 6.834 GHz lower in optical frequency than F=1→F′.

A convenient EOM assignment follows design choice. Lock the carrier near F=1→F′=1 and use it as the strong control; the −1 sideband, 6.834 GHz below, is the weak probe on F=2→F′=1. The control optically pumps atoms out of F=1 into F=2, exactly where the probe starts, which is the weak-probe condition the theory assumes. The +1 sideband lands 6.834 GHz above the control and is off resonance; the paper warns that such off-resonant sidebands can couple in and drive multi-wave mixing. The mirror-image choice (carrier near F=2→F′=1, +1 sideband on F=1→F′=1) also works, and the simulator lets you pick either.

2b · Phase modulation: carrier, sidebands and Bessel weights

An EOM changes the optical phase in proportion to the applied voltage. Driven by a sinusoid at Ω = 2πfmw with phase amplitude β (the modulation depth), the output field is a comb (Jacobi–Anger expansion):

phase modulation\[ E(t)=E_0\,e^{i(\omega_0t+\beta\sin\Omega t)}=E_0\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_n(\beta)\,e^{i(\omega_0+n\Omega)t},\qquad \beta=\pi\frac{V_{\rm peak}}{V_\pi}. \]
Vibrato on a violin note

Read it in wordsWobbling the phase of a single-frequency laser at fmw is mathematically identical to adding new, phase-locked frequencies at ω₀ ± fmw, ± 2fmw, … The Bessel function Jn(β) is the amplitude of the n-th one. Vπ is the voltage that would shift the phase by π.

Jn(β)
Bessel function of order n: the amplitude recipe
β
modulation depth (radians of phase swing)
Ω
2π × microwave frequency
Vπ
half-wave voltage of the EOM at that frequency (datasheet)

AnalogyA violinist’s vibrato wobbles the pitch of one note. A spectrum analyser would show the note plus faint side tones spaced by the wobble rate. Wobble harder (larger β) and more energy moves into the side tones. FM radio works the same way.

Where it failsVibrato is slow and your ear hears one wobbling note. Here the wobble (6.8 GHz) is far faster than the atom’s linewidth, so the atoms really do see separate, sharp frequencies, all locked in phase to the carrier. That phase lock is what makes the Λ coherent.

power fractions\[ \frac{P_n}{P}=J_n^2(\beta),\qquad \sum_n J_n^2(\beta)=1,\qquad \mathrm{CSR}=\frac{J_0^2(\beta)}{J_1^2(\beta)},\qquad J_0^2\approx1-\frac{\beta^2}{2},\ \ J_{\pm1}^2\approx\frac{\beta^2}{4}\ \ (\beta\ll1). \]
Slicing one pie

Read it in wordsThe total power is conserved and only redistributed. Sideband n gets the fraction Jn²(β). The carrier-to-sideband ratio (CSR) compares the carrier with one first-order sideband. At small β the carrier loses ≈ β²/2 and each first sideband gains ≈ β²/4.

AnalogyOne pie (the laser power) is cut into slices. Turning up the RF power makes the centre slice (the carrier) thinner and the side slices fatter. At β ≈ 2.405 the centre slice vanishes entirely (J₀ = 0), and all the power sits in the sidebands.

Where it failsPie slices are independent. Optical sidebands are coherent and keep fixed phases, so the light’s intensity stays constant in time under pure phase modulation. A real EOM also adds a little unwanted amplitude modulation.

Interactive D · Sideband powers vs modulation depthJ₀², J₁², J₂² of the Jacobi–Anger expansion

β = π·Vpeak/Vπ with Vpeak = √(2·50 Ω·PRF). The default Vπ = 3 V is an assumption; read the real value (at 6.8 GHz, not DC) from your EOM datasheet and confirm it with the Fabry–Pérot.

2c · Two-photon detuning is set by the microwave

Carrier and sideband come from the same laser, so their frequency difference is exactly fmw, with the synthesiser’s accuracy. The laser’s own frequency noise (1–2 MHz for a DFB, per the paper) is common to both fields and cancels in δ. For the Λ pair built on sublevels m (F=1, m) ↔ (F=2, m):

two-photon detuning\[ \delta_m=f_{\rm mw}-\nu_{\rm hfs}-m\,(1.40\ \mathrm{kHz/mG})\,B-\delta_{\rm q}\,[m=0],\qquad \nu_{\rm hfs}\approx6.834\,682\,61\ \mathrm{GHz},\qquad \delta_{\rm q}\approx575\ \mathrm{Hz/G^2}\times B^2 . \]
A piano tuner listening for beats

Read it in wordsEIT happens when the microwave frequency equals the atom’s hyperfine splitting for the chosen pair. The m=0 (“clock”) pair moves only quadratically with B. Pairs with m = ±1 move linearly, by ±1.40 kHz per mG (gF = +½ for F=2 and −½ for F=1, so the difference is 1 × µB).

AnalogyA piano tuner sounds a string against a reference fork and turns the peg until the beating stops. fmw is the peg, the atomic splitting is the fork, and the EIT peak is “no beats”. The laser’s own wander is like the whole room’s pitch drifting: it doesn’t matter, because only the difference between string and fork is heard.

Where it failsA piano has one fork. The atom offers three (m = −1, 0, +1) that split apart in a magnetic field, so you can tune to the wrong one.

Numbersνhfs = 6.834 682 610 904 GHz is from Steck Steck. The quadratic clock coefficient ≈ 575 Hz/G² is a commonly quoted value that we have not verified against Steck in this session verify. At 50 mG it amounts to about 1.4 Hz, negligible here. The 1.40 kHz/mG linear coefficient follows from gF ≈ ±½ derived.

2d · Doppler cancellation and Dicke narrowing

Carrier and sideband travel in one beam, so they are automatically co-propagating and perfectly overlapped. The residual two-photon wavevector is the frequency difference divided by c:

§III A–B\[ |\vec k_p-\vec k_c|=\frac{2\pi\,\nu_{\rm hfs}}{c}\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda_{2\,\rm ph}=\frac{c}{\nu_{\rm hfs}}\approx4.39\ \mathrm{cm},\qquad \gamma_{\rm Dicke}=D\,|\vec k_p-\vec k_c|^2\approx2\pi\times3\ \mathrm{Hz}\ \ (D\approx10\ \mathrm{cm^2/s}). \]
Two sirens on one ambulance, and a crowd that slows it

Read it in wordsEach atom sees both fields Doppler-shifted. Because they travel together and differ by only 6.8 GHz out of 377 THz, the shifts almost cancel in the difference. What remains corresponds to a long “two-photon wavelength” of about 4.4 cm. With buffer gas, atoms diffuse so slowly over 4.4 cm that this residual broadening collapses to a few Hz. The paper quotes “a few Hz” for 10 Torr buffer gas.

AnalogyTwo sirens bolted to the same ambulance shift in pitch together, so the musical interval between them never changes. In a crowded street (buffer gas) the ambulance hardly moves in the time it takes to hear one “cycle” of the interval.

Where it failsThe cancellation is not perfect (a 4.4 cm, not infinite, wavelength). A beam angle reintroduces Doppler broadening, and other mechanisms (transit, power, fields) usually dominate long before the Hz-level Dicke limit.

Arithmeticc/νhfs = 2.998×10⁸ / 6.8347×10⁹ ≈ 4.39 cm. k = 2π/4.39 cm ≈ 1.43 cm⁻¹, so Dk² ≈ 20 s⁻¹ ≈ 2π × 3.3 Hz with the paper’s D ≈ 10 cm²/s derived.

2e · Magnetic fields, the clock pair and why a small bias is used

With light propagating along B and both fields carrying the same circular polarization (they share one beam), the Λ links ground sublevels with the same m: (F=1, m) ↔ (F=2, m) via m′ = m+1. That gives three Λ resonances, m = −1, 0, +1. The m=0 “clock” pair has no first-order Zeeman shift. In zero field all three overlap, and any residual field or gradient smears them into one broad, distorted line. The paper explains the remedy: a well-defined field (typically 10–100 mG) “separates the Zeeman sub-levels”, which is advantageous for hyperfine EIT when only one transition (e.g. the clock transition mF=0) is to be addressed.

Background (not from the paper): with circular polarization, optical pumping also pushes population toward an extreme sublevel that takes part in no Λ (a “trap” state), reducing contrast. CPT-clock work uses polarization schemes such as push–pull optical pumping (paper ref. [104]) to counter this. Treat it as a known complication to read up on before optimising contrast.

Zeeman pairs\[ \Delta\nu_m=(g_{F=2}-g_{F=1})\,m\,\frac{\mu_BB}{h}\approx m\times1.40\ \mathrm{MHz/G}=m\times1.40\ \mathrm{kHz/mG},\qquad m\in\{-1,0,+1\}. \]
Three strings, one of them field-proof

Read it in wordsA field B splits the hyperfine resonance into three. The outer two move apart in proportion to B and the middle one barely moves. A small bias deliberately pulls the outer two away so the clock line stands alone.

AnalogyImagine three guitar strings where heating the room sharpens one, flattens another and leaves the middle one in tune. If they all sound together while the temperature fluctuates, you hear mush. Detune the outer two on purpose (bias field) and you can listen to the stable middle one cleanly.

Where it failsThe “middle string” is not perfectly immune: it shifts quadratically, and field gradients across the cell still dephase it slightly.

2f · The detection problem: probe and control share one mode

The paper is explicit: with an EOM, “the carrier and all modulation sidebands emerge perfectly spatially overlapped, so that either the experiment must operate with the control and probe fields in the same spatial mode and polarization, or additional filtering must be employed to separate the desired modulation sideband.” A photodiode after the cell therefore sees carrier + both sidebands + higher orders. EIT changes only the probe sideband’s transmission, so the signal is a small bump on a large, mostly constant background. No PBS or Glan–Taylor can help, because all components share one polarization.

Options to investigate to verify for our setup:

  • Lock-in detection. Dither fmw (frequency modulation of the synthesiser) and demodulate the photodiode at the dither frequency. The constant background drops out and you get the derivative of the EIT line, whose zero crossing marks resonance. This is standard practice in CPT and EIT spectroscopy; the simulator’s “Lock-in” mode shows the idea.
  • Spectral filtering. A Fabry–Pérot etalon whose transmission passes the probe sideband and rejects the carrier 6.834 GHz away, or a filter cell, before the detector. Filter-cell schemes need care (they absorb on the same lines), so check the literature before buying.
  • Balanced or differential detection against a reference beam that bypasses the cell, to suppress laser intensity noise on the large background.
Hearing a whisper at a concert

Read it in wordsThe detector reports total power. The EIT signal is a percent-level change of one weak spectral component inside a much brighter beam.

AnalogyTrying to hear a friend whisper during a concert: the whisper is there, but buried. A lock-in is like your friend tapping a rhythm you both agreed on, so you listen only for that rhythm. A spectral filter is earplugs that block the band (the carrier) and leave the whisper’s pitch.

Where it failsEarplugs cannot tell two notes 6.8 GHz apart within a 377 THz carrier without a very sharp optical filter, and a lock-in only helps if the noise is not at the dither frequency.

B3

Mod B architecture & timing

One beam, one modulator, one cell. Counterpart: Mod A architecture.

1 · Source, isolation, lock

Identical to Mod A’s lock loop (SAS photodiode → lock box → laser controller → DFB current, TEC for slow drift). The lock point is chosen so the carrier sits on the control transition (near F=1→F′=1 in our assignment) design choice. Pick off the SAS light before the EOM, and if the laser lock uses a current dither, give it a different frequency from the microwave dither used for EIT readout.

2 · Polarization into the EOM

Fibre EOMs are polarization-maintaining and modulate one crystal axis. A PBS before the fibre coupler, plus a λ/2 for alignment, sets clean linear polarization on that axis. Off-axis light is not modulated and adds background.

3 · Microwave chain

Synthesiser at ≈ 6.834 683 GHz with fine resolution and FM capability, an amplifier sized to reach the target β (see Interactive D) within the EOM’s RF rating, SMA cabling and an optional RF switch for gating. Synthesiser phase noise appears directly in δ.

4 · Sideband check

A glass pick-off sends a few percent to a scanning Fabry–Pérot. Measure the CSR vs RF power, compare with J₀²/J₁², and extract the real Vπ.

5 · Cell, field, shield

Same cell, heater and TC300B as Mod A. A λ/4 makes all components σ⁺. The solenoid supplies a 10–100 mG bias (paper) to isolate the clock pair, inside a degaussed shield.

6 · Detection

Photodiode on total power, read through a lock-in referenced to an fmw dither, plus a scope for sweeps. Spectral filtering is an upgrade to investigate (see 2f).

Light storage in Mod B: options to verify

What the paper says, and candidate schemes. None of the schemes below is claimed as established for our setup.

What the paper supports
  • EOM carrier and sidebands are perfectly spatially overlapped, so you either operate in the same mode and polarization or add filtering to separate the desired sideband (Sec. V A).
  • Off-resonant modulation sidebands can couple into the atoms, for example leading to undesirable multi-wave mixing (Sec. V, citing ref. [73]).
  • The paper cites refs. [83] (optimal light storage with full pulse-shape control) and [84] (EIT quantum memory with Cs vapour) where it recommends EOMs for high modulation frequencies. Read them for how the fields were generated and separated; we have not extracted those details.
  • Far-detuned Raman memories are an alternative to EIT memories (Sec. II B).

Why Mod A stores more easily

Read it in wordsStorage needs the weak probe on the detector and the strong control gone. In Mod A they differ in polarization. In Mod B they differ only in colour, by 6.8 GHz out of 377 THz.

AnalogyMod A is mail in red and blue envelopes: sorting is trivial. Mod B is identical envelopes whose only difference is a faint watermark, so you need a precise optical “watermark reader” (an etalon) before you can sort.

3D bench simulator: Mod B

Same engine as the Mod A simulator, now with a working EOM. Each ray carries a spectrum (carrier + sidebands, same polarization), and the cell responds per spectral component.

How to use it. As in Mod A: drag from the palette, click to select, R to rotate, Del to delete. Select the EOM to set the microwave frequency and RF power and to read the Bessel weights. Select the Fabry–Pérot to see the spectrum. Select the SAS to pick which line the carrier is locked to. The scope’s Lock-in button shows the derivative signal. The bench opens with the Mod B reference layout.

Model honesty
  • The EOM uses exact Jacobi–Anger weights for orders up to ±3. β comes from RF power with an assumed Vπ = 3 V, insertion transmission 0.8 (illustrative), and only the H component is modulated.
  • A component counts as “on a D1 line” within ±300 MHz. The strongest on-line component is the control; the strongest on the other hyperfine line is the probe.
  • Probe transmission: exp[−OD(1 − C Σ wm L(δm))]. It uses three Λ pairs at m × 1.40 kHz/mG × B, illustrative strengths (circular 0.6/1/0.6, linear 0.3/0.5/0.3), and the Mod A linewidth fit borrowed as an illustrative width.
  • Off-resonant components pass unchanged: no multi-wave mixing, no light shifts, no trap-state pumping.
  • The detector reports total power over all components. Storage is not simulated for Mod B.
  • The laser lock is the same SAS → lock box → laser controller → DFB chain as in Mod A. An open loop lets the laser drift, which shifts every spectral component together.

      Bring-up & audit (Mod B)

      Assumes the laser, isolator, SAS lock and cell temperature control are already commissioned as in Mod A O1.1–O1.7. Pass criteria are design targets.

      1. EOM input. Couple into the EOM’s PM fibre with polarization on the specified axis (λ/2 before the coupler). Rotate the input λ/2 while watching output polarization with a polariser. Pass · polarization extinction at the output as specified by the datasheet; coupling efficiency recorded; optical power below the EOM’s rating.
      2. Microwave chain. Synthesiser → amplifier → (switch) → EOM, with attenuators and a power meter or spectrum analyser at the EOM input. Never exceed the EOM RF rating. Pass · fmw stable and settable to ≤ 1 Hz resolution; RF power at the EOM known in dBm.
      3. Sideband verification. Scan the Fabry–Pérot. Record carrier, ±1 and ±2 heights vs RF power, fit Jn²(β), and extract Vπ at 6.8 GHz. Pass · measured ratios follow Bessel curves; chosen β gives the intended CSR (e.g. ~10:1 at β ≈ 0.6).
      4. Lock point. With modulation on, scan the laser slowly in open loop and identify the Doppler absorption from carrier and sidebands in the cell. Then close the SAS loop as in Mod A step O1.5 (open-loop scan, find lines, set slope sign, close, check), with the carrier on the control transition. Pass · lock holds with modulation applied; error signal near zero; laser and microwave dithers at different frequencies.
      5. Field. Degauss the shield, apply a 10–100 mG bias, and set λ/4 for circular polarization. Pass · three resonances (m = −1, 0, +1) resolved, outer ones spaced by 1.40 kHz/mG × B from the clock line.
      6. CW hyperfine EIT. Sweep fmw by ± a few hundred kHz around 6.834 683 GHz, detecting total power and lock-in X. Pass · clock resonance visible on the background; lock-in zero crossing within a few Hz of νhfs after quadratic correction.
      7. Width and OD study. Measure FWHM vs control power and temperature and compare with Mod A on the same cell. Pass · consistent decoherence floor between Mod A and Mod B once field effects are accounted for.
      8. (Optional) storage feasibility. Only after one of the storage options has been checked in the literature and its filter procured. Pass · filtered probe detectable above carrier leakage at the intended power level.
