L1

Light as waves and photons

Learning goals
  • Describe light as an electromagnetic wave with wavelength, frequency and wavevector.
  • Explain energy quantization and compute the photon energy at 795 nm.
  • Convert optical power into photon rate, the bridge between “mW” and “single photons”.

Classically, a laser beam is an oscillating electric field. At 795 nm it oscillates about 3.8 × 10¹⁴ times per second, far too fast for any detector to follow, so detectors measure time-averaged power. Quantum mechanically, that energy arrives in indivisible packets, photons, each carrying hν. Both pictures are right. The wave picture explains interference and polarization (the waveplates of Method A); the photon picture explains why a detector clicks and why a quantum memory must preserve single quanta.

L1.1\[ E(z,t)=E_0\cos(kz-\omega t),\qquad k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda},\quad \omega=2\pi\nu,\quad c=\lambda\nu;\qquad E_{\rm photon}=h\nu=\frac{hc}{\lambda},\qquad \Phi=\frac{P}{h\nu}. \]
Rain and raindrops

Read it in wordsThe field is a travelling wave with spatial period λ and temporal frequency ν. Its energy comes in lumps of hν, and the photon flux Φ is power divided by the energy per lump.

AnalogyOptical power is like rainfall in mm per hour; photons are the individual raindrops. Heavy rain looks continuous, but in a drizzle you can count the drops. A “single-photon-level” probe is a drizzle so light you count individual drops.

Where it failsRaindrops have positions before they land. A photon has no well-defined trajectory until it is detected, and it can interfere with itself.

Worked example · 795 nm

λ = 794.979 nm Steck ⇒ ν = c/λ ≈ 377.107 THz. E = hc/λ = (6.626×10⁻³⁴ J s)(2.998×10⁸ m/s)/(7.950×10⁻⁷ m) ≈ 2.499 × 10⁻¹⁹ J ≈ 1.560 eV.

A 1 µW probe carries 10⁻⁶ / 2.499×10⁻¹⁹ ≈ 4.0 × 10¹² photons/s, and 1 nW carries 4.0 × 10⁹ photons/s. A 10 µs pulse at 1 µW holds ≈ 4 × 10⁷ photons: a long way from one.

Interactive · photon counterE = hc/λ, Φ = P/E
Q1. How many photons per second are in a 1 pW beam at 795 nm?
1×10⁻¹² W / 2.499×10⁻¹⁹ J ≈ 4.0 × 10⁶ photons/s. Even a picowatt is millions of photons per second.
Q2. Why can the photodiode in Method A not see individual 795 nm photons?
An ordinary photodiode with a transimpedance amplifier integrates current. Its noise is equivalent to many photons per measurement time, so single quanta are hidden. Counting single photons needs single-photon detectors (e.g. avalanche photodiodes), which are not in the current equipment plan.

L2

Quantum states, superposition and measurement

Learning goals
  • Write states in Dirac notation and normalise them.
  • Distinguish probability amplitudes from probabilities.
  • See why relative phase is physical and evolves in time.

A quantum state is a vector |ψ⟩. For a two-state system with basis {|g⟩, |e⟩}, any state is a superposition with complex amplitudes. Measurement in that basis gives g or e with probabilities |cg|² and |ce|², and leaves the system in the outcome state. The relative phase between cg and ce does not change those probabilities, but it decides how the state interferes later. EIT is built entirely on such a phase (L7).

L2.1\[ |\psi\rangle=c_g|g\rangle+c_e|e\rangle,\qquad |c_g|^2+|c_e|^2=1,\qquad P(e)=|\langle e|\psi\rangle|^2=|c_e|^2,\qquad c_j(t)=c_j(0)\,e^{-iE_jt/\hbar}. \]
A compass needle and a yes/no question

Read it in wordsThe state is a weighted, phased mixture of basis states. Probabilities are squared magnitudes. Each component’s phase rotates at its own energy, so the relative phase rotates at the energy difference divided by ħ.

AnalogyA compass needle can point anywhere, but a measurement only asks “north or south?”. A needle at 45° answers north half the time. The relative phase is like the needle swinging east–west: invisible to a north/south question, but it changes what a later east/west question would give.

Where it failsA real needle has a definite direction before you look. A quantum state does not have a definite outcome, and measuring it changes the state.

Worked example · hyperfine superposition

Prepare (|F=1⟩ + |F=2⟩)/√2 in ⁸⁷Rb. The relative phase advances at νhfs ≈ 6.834 683 GHz: a full turn every 1/νhfs ≈ 146.3 ps. Method B’s microwave frequency must track that rotation; Method A’s Zeeman superposition rotates only at kHz (70 kHz at 50 mG, a 14 µs period).

Q1. Is (|g⟩ + i|e⟩)/√2 different from (|g⟩ + |e⟩)/√2 if you only measure in the {g, e} basis?
No. Both give 50/50. They differ in relative phase (90°), which shows up when the state interferes with something else, e.g. after another light pulse.
Q2. Why does a global phase eiφ|ψ⟩ not matter, while a relative phase does?
All probabilities involve |amplitude|², which is unchanged by a common factor eiφ. A relative phase changes how the amplitudes add when they are projected onto a different basis.

L3

The two-level atom: dipole coupling, RWA, Rabi oscillations, Bloch sphere

Learning goals
  • Write the dipole Hamiltonian and apply the rotating-wave approximation.
  • Predict Rabi oscillations, including the effect of detuning.
  • Picture the state on the Bloch sphere.

An atom with transition frequency ω₀ in a laser field E cos ωt feels the energy −d·E. Moving to a frame rotating at ω and dropping terms oscillating at 2ω (the RWA) leaves a simple 2×2 problem that depends only on the detuning Δ = ω − ω₀ and the Rabi frequency Ω. Here Ω = d·E₀/ħ is the full Rabi frequency, the rate of population cycling on resonance. The EIT paper defines Ω = µẼ/ħ with its own field convention; always check factors of 2 when comparing sources.

L3.1\[ \hat H_{\rm RWA}=-\hbar\Delta\,|e\rangle\langle e|+\frac{\hbar\Omega}{2}\big(|e\rangle\langle g|+|g\rangle\langle e|\big),\qquad P_e(t)=\frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}\,\sin^2\!\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}}{2}\,t\Big). \]
Pushing a swing in rhythm, or slightly off-beat

Read it in wordsOn resonance (Δ = 0) the population swings fully between g and e at rate Ω. Off resonance it swings faster (at √(Ω²+Δ²)) but never fully reaches e: the maximum is Ω²/(Ω²+Δ²).

AnalogyPush a swing exactly at its natural rhythm and it goes higher and higher. Push slightly off-beat (detuning) and some pushes fight the motion: the swing goes up a little, comes back, and never reaches full height.

Where it failsA swing’s amplitude can grow without limit. The atom’s population cannot exceed 1, so it swings back down even on resonance: g → e → g. That is coherent stimulated emission back into the laser.

L3.2\[ \vec r=(u,v,w),\quad w=\rho_{ee}-\rho_{gg},\qquad \dot{\vec r}=\vec\Omega_{\rm eff}\times\vec r\ \ \text{(no decay)},\qquad \vec\Omega_{\rm eff}=(\Omega,0,-\Delta). \]
A spinning top precessing

Read it in wordsAny two-level state is a point on a sphere: north pole is |e⟩, south pole is |g⟩, and the equator holds equal superpositions with different phases. The laser makes the point rotate about an axis set by Ω and Δ.

AnalogyA top’s axis precesses around gravity; the Bloch vector precesses around the “laser axis”. Detuning tilts that axis, so the vector traces a smaller circle that never reaches the north pole.

Where it failsOpposite points on the Bloch sphere are orthogonal states, not opposite directions in space. With decay the vector also shrinks inside the sphere (L4).

Interactive · Rabi oscillation & Bloch sphereoptical Bloch equations, RK4, starts in |g⟩

Equations: u̇ = Δv − (Γ/2)u, v̇ = −Δu + Ωw − (Γ/2)v, ẇ = −Ωv − Γ(w+1). With decay on, oscillations damp toward a steady state: the reason continuous lasers on a two-level atom give absorption rather than endless Rabi cycling.

Worked example · how big is Ω in Method A?

A common two-level estimate is Ω = Γ√(I/(2Isat)). With Γ/2π = 5.746 MHz and the D1 far-detuned π-polarized Isat ≈ 4.484 mW/cm² Steck, the paper’s 1.3 mW/cm² control gives Ω/2π ≈ 5.746 × √(1.3/8.968) ≈ 2.2 MHz. The paper quotes Ωc/2π = 1.9 MHz: the same order, with the difference coming from Clebsch–Gordan factors and Rabi-frequency conventions. A π-pulse at Ω/2π = 2 MHz takes π/Ω = 250 ns.

Q1. With Δ = Ω, what is the maximum excited population?
Ω²/(Ω²+Ω²) = 1/2.
Q2. Why is the RWA safe for Rb D1 driven by a mW laser?
The dropped terms oscillate at ≈ 2 × 377 THz, while Ω and Δ are MHz to GHz. Their effect averages out (the Bloch–Siegert shift is of order Ω²/ω, utterly negligible here).

L4

Open systems: density matrix, decay and dephasing

Learning goals
  • Use the density matrix for mixed states.
  • Distinguish populations from coherences.
  • Read a Lindblad master equation and identify Γ, Γ/2 and pure dephasing.

Real atoms leak energy (spontaneous emission) and lose phase (collisions, fields, motion). A state vector cannot describe a statistical mixture, but a density matrix can. Diagonal elements are populations (how many atoms are where); off-diagonal elements are coherences (how well-defined the relative phase is across the ensemble). EIT and memories live in coherences, so coherence lifetimes are the central figure of merit of the whole project.

L4.1\[ \hat\rho=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{ee}&\rho_{eg}\\ \rho_{ge}&\rho_{gg}\end{pmatrix},\qquad \dot{\hat\rho}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat H,\hat\rho]+\Gamma\Big(\hat\sigma\hat\rho\hat\sigma^\dagger-\tfrac12\{\hat\sigma^\dagger\hat\sigma,\hat\rho\}\Big),\qquad \hat\sigma=|g\rangle\langle e|. \]
An audience clapping in time

Read it in wordsPopulations decay at Γ. The coherence ρeg decays at Γ/2 from emission alone, plus any extra pure-dephasing rate. The commutator is the reversible part; the Lindblad term is irreversible.

AnalogyAn audience clapping in unison is a coherence: everyone shares a phase. People leaving the hall is population decay. People staying but drifting off the beat is dephasing: the hall still holds the same number of clappers, but the rhythm (coherence) is gone.

Where it failsEach atom is itself in a superposition, not just “clapping or not”. Dephasing can be partly reversible (inhomogeneous, e.g. Doppler) or truly irreversible (homogeneous). The audience picture blurs that distinction.

Worked example · from lifetime to linewidth

τ(5P₁/₂) = 27.70 ns Steck ⇒ Γ = 1/τ = 3.61 × 10⁷ s⁻¹ ⇒ Γ/2π = 5.746 MHz, matching Steck’s quoted natural linewidth. The optical coherence decays at Γ/2 (γ₁₃ ≈ 2π × 2.87 MHz without collisions). With 10 Torr of buffer gas, pressure broadening (~10 MHz/Torr, paper) dominates, at ~100 MHz.

Q1. A state has ρee = ρgg = ½ and ρeg = 0. Is it a superposition?
No. It is a 50/50 statistical mixture with no coherence. A superposition (|g⟩+|e⟩)/√2 would have |ρeg| = ½.
Q2. Why is γ₁₂ (ground coherence) potentially millions of times smaller than γ₁₃?
Ground states do not decay radiatively. γ₁₂ comes only from slow processes (transit, collisions, field gradients), typically kHz or less, while γ₁₃ includes the MHz radiative decay and collisional broadening.

L5

Atomic structure for alkalis

Learning goals
  • Build fine, hyperfine and Zeeman structure from angular-momentum addition.
  • Apply dipole selection rules for σ⁺, σ⁻ and π light.
  • Locate the Method A and Method B Λ systems in ⁸⁷Rb.

Rubidium’s single valence electron has orbital L and spin S = ½, which combine to J (fine structure: 5P₁/₂ vs 5P₃/₂, the D1 vs D2 lines). The nucleus has spin I = 3/2 for ⁸⁷Rb, and J and I combine to F (hyperfine). In a field B each F splits into 2F+1 sublevels mF. Light carries angular momentum: σ⁺ adds +1 to mF, σ⁻ subtracts 1, and π (polarized along B) adds 0.

L5.1\[ |I-J|\le F\le I+J\ \Rightarrow\ F\in\{1,2\}\ (I=\tfrac32,\ J=\tfrac12),\qquad \Delta E_{\rm Z}=g_F\,\mu_B\,m_F\,B,\qquad \Delta F=0,\pm1,\quad \Delta m_F=0\ (\pi),\ \pm1\ (\sigma^\pm). \]
A postal address

Read it in wordsPossible F values run from |I−J| to I+J in integer steps. Each sublevel shifts in energy by gFµBmFB. Absorbing a photon can change F by at most one and mF by the photon’s angular momentum.

AnalogyAn address narrows down step by step: building (n, L), floor (J), apartment (F), room (mF). The magnetic field is a lift that raises rooms on one side of the corridor and lowers those on the other. Selection rules are the doors: σ⁺ light only opens the door to the next room to the right.

Where it failsRooms are places. These are angular-momentum states, and a superposition can be “in several rooms” at once, which is what Λ-EIT exploits.

Worked example · counting and shifting

⁸⁷Rb 5S₁/₂ has 3 + 5 = 8 sublevels. With gF ≈ +½ (F=2), the shift is 0.70 MHz/G per mF Steck. Earth’s field (roughly half a gauss) would shift mF=+2 by about 0.7 MHz, enormous compared with a 10 kHz EIT line. That is why the cell sits inside µ-metal. Method A uses the mF=+2/0 pair (1.40 kHz/mG); Method B uses F=1/F=2 clock pairs (m=0↔0, quadratic only).

Q1. Why can σ⁺ light on F=2→F′=1 never excite mF=+2?
It would need mF′=+3, which does not exist in F′=1 (maximum +1). mF=+1 is also dark, since F′=1 has no mF′=+2.
Q2. Why is D1 preferred over D2 for clean Λ systems?
5P₁/₂ has only two excited hyperfine levels (F′=1, 2), well resolved compared with 5P₃/₂’s four. That means fewer competing excitation paths and cleaner dark states.

L6

Light–matter response: susceptibility, absorption, dispersion

Learning goals
  • Relate χ to refractive index and absorption.
  • Use Beer’s law and optical depth.
  • State the Kramers–Kronig link between absorption and dispersion.

Many atoms together respond to light with a polarization P = ε₀χE. The real part of χ slows the phase (refractive index); the imaginary part removes energy (absorption). Beer’s law follows: intensity falls exponentially with distance, and the exponent at resonance is the optical depth.

L6.1\[ \chi=\chi'+i\chi'',\qquad n\simeq1+\tfrac12\chi',\qquad I(L)=I_0\,e^{-\mathrm{OD}},\ \ \mathrm{OD}=\int_0^L n_{\rm at}\,\sigma\,dz,\qquad \chi'(\omega)=\frac{2}{\pi}\,\mathcal P\!\!\int_0^\infty\frac{\omega'\chi''(\omega')}{\omega'^2-\omega^2}\,d\omega'. \]
Tinted glass, and a road with a dip

Read it in wordsχ′ bends the phase (index); χ″ absorbs. OD is atom density × cross-section × length, summed along the path. Kramers–Kronig says χ′ at any frequency is fixed by χ″ at all frequencies: you cannot have one without the other.

AnalogyOD counts sheets of tinted glass, each passing 1/e. For Kramers–Kronig, think of a road with a dip: wherever the surface dips (absorption line), the slope on either side (dispersion) must go down and come back up. A narrow dip means steep slopes.

Where it failsGlass absorbs broadly. Atoms absorb in narrow lines, and the “dip” can be inverted (EIT carves a transparency hole) while the same causal link still produces steep dispersion.

Lab connection: keeping the laser on the lineThe same absorption physics gives the laser its reference. In saturated absorption spectroscopy a strong pump burns narrow sub-Doppler dips into a reference cell’s absorption. A lock box turns the dip into a dispersive error signal (zero on the line, opposite signs either side, like a derivative of the dip) and feeds a correction back to the laser current. See the SAS → lock box → laser controller → DFB loop.
Worked example · how opaque is the cell?

The paper’s cell had OD ~10 at ~60 °C paper. Without EIT, T = e⁻¹⁰ ≈ 4.5 × 10⁻⁵, so 1 µW in gives 45 pW out. Our vapour-pressure estimate gives n ≈ 3 × 10¹¹ cm⁻³ at 60 °C design estimate, inside the paper’s typical 10¹¹–10¹² cm⁻³.

Q1. OD doubles from 5 to 10. By what factor does resonant transmission change?
e⁻¹⁰/e⁻⁵ = e⁻⁵ ≈ 6.7 × 10⁻³: about 150 times less light.
Q2. Why does a narrow absorption feature imply a strong dispersion feature?
Kramers–Kronig: the refractive-index change scales with the absorption strength divided by its width, so narrower features give steeper index slopes. This is the root of slow light under EIT.

L7

Three-level interference: dark states, CPT, EIT, Autler–Townes

Learning goals
  • Compare Λ, V and ladder schemes.
  • Derive the dark state and the EIT window.
  • Distinguish EIT (interference) from Autler–Townes splitting (level shifting).

Add a second ground state and a second laser. Two excitation routes now lead to the same excited state, and their amplitudes can cancel. The cancelling superposition is the dark state. If the control is strong and the probe weak, the probe sees a narrow transparency window: EIT. With two comparable fields, atoms are trapped in the dark state: CPT. A strong control also splits the excited state into an Autler–Townes doublet ±Ωc, which also reduces absorption at line centre, but without interference, so some residual absorption remains (paper refs [29], [30]).

L7.1\[ |D\rangle=\frac{\Omega_p|2\rangle-\Omega_c|1\rangle}{\sqrt{|\Omega_p|^2+|\Omega_c|^2}},\qquad \gamma_{\rm EIT}=\gamma_{12}+\frac{|\Omega_c|^2}{\gamma_{13}},\qquad E_\pm=\pm\hbar\Omega_c\ \ (\text{Autler–Townes, paper Eq. 23}). \]
Two pushes that cancel; noise-cancelling headphones

Read it in wordsThe dark state mixes |1⟩ and |2⟩ with weights set by the opposite field and a minus sign. The transparency window has a width set by ground decoherence plus control power. Autler–Townes describes the control splitting the excited level into two.

AnalogyTwo friends push a swing from opposite sides with matched rhythm: it stays still. The window is like noise-cancelling headphones: silence only in a band. Autler–Townes is different: it is like moving a single pothole into two smaller potholes on either side of your wheel. You miss them both, but not because anything cancelled.

Where it failsNo light is added to cancel the probe; the cancellation is between quantum amplitudes inside each atom. Real experiments often sit between pure EIT and pure Autler–Townes, and distinguishing them takes careful fitting.

Interactive · the EIT windowpaper Eq. 13 with γ₁₃ → W (effective optical width)
transmissionphase (right axis)no control
Worked example · width at the paper’s control power

Ωc/2π = 1.9 MHz, effective optical width W/2π ≈ 300 MHz design estimate, γ₁₂/2π = 1 kHz. Then γEIT/2π ≈ 1 + 1900²/300 000 kHz ≈ 13 kHz (HWHM), i.e. FWHM ≈ 26 kHz. The paper measured 7.3 kHz FWHM at this control intensity, so the crude formula overestimates here by a factor of ~3. That is a reminder to measure, not assume.

Q1. In the dark state with Ωp ≪ Ωc, where is almost all the population?
In |1⟩, the state coupled by the weak probe. Weight of |1⟩ = |Ωc|²/(|Ωc|²+|Ωp|²) ≈ 1. That is exactly why the result is counter-intuitive.
Q2. Halving the control intensity halves which term of γEIT?
The power-broadening term |Ωc|²/γ₁₃ (Ω² ∝ intensity). γ₁₂ is unchanged.

L8

Slow and stopped light: group velocity, polaritons, storage

Learning goals
  • Compute group velocity and delay from an EIT spectrum.
  • Describe the dark-state polariton and its mixing angle.
  • Explain storage and retrieval as adiabatic transfer between light and spin wave.

The steep dispersion inside the EIT window makes the group index huge, so a pulse crawls through the cell. In the Fleischhauer–Lukin picture (paper ref. [11]) the propagating excitation is a dark-state polariton, a superposition of the probe field and the collective spin coherence. Its mixing angle θ is set by the control. Lower the control adiabatically to zero and θ → 90°: the excitation becomes purely atomic and stops. Raise it again and the light returns.

L8.1\[ v_g=\frac{c}{n_g}\approx c\cos^2\theta,\qquad \hat\Psi=\cos\theta\,\hat{\mathcal E}-\sin\theta\,\sqrt{N}\,\hat\rho_{12},\qquad \tan^2\theta=\frac{g^2N}{\Omega_c^2},\qquad \tau_{\rm delay}=\frac{\mathrm{OD}_{\rm EIT}}{\gamma_{\rm EIT}}. \]
Traffic on a crowded road, and a relay baton

Read it in wordsThe polariton Ψ is part light (cos θ) and part atomic spin wave (sin θ). g√N is the collective light–atom coupling, so more atoms means more atomic character. Group velocity falls as the excitation becomes more atomic. The delay follows from the measured ODEIT and γEIT.

AnalogyA convoy entering a crowded road bunches up and slows: that is pulse compression. Storage is a relay race: the light runner hands the baton (the quantum state) to an atom runner who stands still holding it until the control says “go”.

Where it failsThe convoy loses no cars, and the baton does not fade. In the atoms, energy is temporarily held by the atoms and control field, and the stored spin wave decays (γ₁₂) and diffuses while waiting.

Worked example · slow light in numbers

Take ODEIT ≈ 9.6 and γEIT/2π ≈ 21 kHz (the simulator’s Method A reference, illustrative). τ = 9.6/(2π × 21 × 10³ s⁻¹) ≈ 73 µs. In an assumed 7.5 cm cell that is vg ≈ 0.075 m / 73 µs ≈ 1 km/s, about 3 × 10⁵ times slower than c. A 47 µs pulse is 14 km long in vacuum but ≈ 5 cm inside the cell: it fits, so it can be stored.

Q1. What happens to vg if you halve the control intensity (power-broadening-dominated regime)?
γEIT roughly halves, so the delay ODEIT/γEIT roughly doubles and vg roughly halves. Transparency (ODEIT) may also drop, which is the trade-off.
Q2. Why must the control be turned off while the pulse is fully inside the cell?
Only the part of the pulse inside the medium is converted to spin wave. Anything already out (or not yet in) is lost or unstored. That is why the delay must exceed the pulse length (time-bandwidth product ≈ √ODEIT > 1).

L9

Thermal vapour realities: Doppler, transit, collisions, buffer gas

Learning goals
  • Estimate Doppler width and transit time for warm Rb.
  • Explain why co-propagating Λ beams cancel Doppler broadening.
  • Describe what a buffer gas buys and what it costs.

Atoms in a warm cell move at hundreds of metres per second. Each motional effect is a time-of-flight T across a length l, giving broadening ≈ 1/T (paper Eq. 29). Over an optical wavelength this is Doppler broadening; across the beam it is transit broadening; across the two-photon wavelength it determines whether EIT survives motion.

L9.1\[ v_{\rm th}=\sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{m}},\qquad \sigma_{\rm Dop}=v_{\rm th}k,\qquad \Gamma_{tt}\sim\frac{v_{\rm th}}{w_0}\ \text{(ballistic)},\ \ \frac{D}{w_0^2}\ \text{(diffusive)},\qquad \delta_{\rm Dop}^{(2)}=(\vec k_p-\vec k_c)\cdot\vec v. \]
A siren, a spotlight and a crowded room

Read it in wordsThermal speed sets the Doppler width. Beam size and speed (or diffusion) set the time in the beam. The two-photon Doppler shift depends only on the difference of the two wavevectors.

AnalogyA passing ambulance siren changes pitch (Doppler), but two sirens on the same ambulance keep the same interval (co-propagating Λ). An atom crossing the beam is a runner crossing a spotlight: fast runners are lit only briefly. Buffer gas turns the stage into a crowded room, where the runner staggers about and stays in the light far longer.

Where it failsBuffer-gas collisions also shift and broaden the optical line (pressure broadening), and very high pressure eventually destroys ground coherence too.

Worked example · ⁸⁷Rb at 60 °C

m = 86.909 u Steck, T = 333 K ⇒ vth ≈ 179 m/s. σDop/2π = vth/λ ≈ 225 MHz (the paper’s Fig. 6 uses 230 MHz). Ballistic crossing of a 0.75 mm beam radius takes ≈ 4.2 µs. With D ≈ 10 cm²/s (paper, 10 Torr), diffusion across it takes w₀²/D ≈ 0.56 ms, about 130 times longer derived.

Q1. Why does a ladder (cascade) scheme prefer counter-propagating beams, while Λ prefers co-propagating?
In Λ one photon is absorbed and one emitted, so the effective wavevector is kp − kc, which is small when the beams travel together. In a ladder both photons are absorbed, so it is kp + kc, which is minimised by opposite directions (paper Fig. 9 inset).
Q2. A beam angle of 0.1 mrad between probe and control: roughly how much residual broadening without buffer gas?
σDopθ ≈ 225 MHz × 10⁻⁴ ≈ 20 kHz: “tens of kHz”, as the paper says. With buffer gas, Dicke narrowing makes it quadratic in θ and much smaller.

L10

Quantum memories: metrics, repeaters, networks

Learning goals
  • Define efficiency, fidelity, storage time, bandwidth and multimode capacity.
  • Explain why repeaters need memories.
  • Place the project’s Rb platform among memory goals.

A quantum memory stores a quantum state of light and releases it on demand. Photon loss in fibre grows exponentially with distance and a quantum state cannot be copied, so long-distance links need repeaters. Repeaters create entanglement over short segments and connect them, and they need memories to hold one segment’s success while the others try. The DLCZ proposal (paper ref. [47]) used atomic ensembles for exactly this. Published Rb and Cs ensemble memories report high efficiencies and fidelities (e.g. Hsiao et al. 2018, paper ref. [114]); read the originals for the numbers.

L10.1\[ \eta=\frac{E_{\rm out}}{E_{\rm in}},\qquad \mathcal F=|\langle\psi_{\rm in}|\psi_{\rm out}\rangle|^2\ \ (\text{pure states}),\qquad \eta(\tau)\approx\eta_0\,e^{-\tau/T_{\rm mem}},\qquad 1-\eta_0\propto\frac{1}{\mathrm{OD}_{\rm EIT}},\qquad B\tau_{\rm delay}\approx\sqrt{\mathrm{OD}_{\rm EIT}}. \]
A left-luggage office

Read it in wordsEfficiency is how much of the stored energy (or how many photons) comes back. Fidelity is how faithfully the state comes back. Memory time is how long before efficiency decays. Inefficiency shrinks with OD (paper). The time–bandwidth product says how many pulses fit in the medium.

AnalogyA left-luggage office. Efficiency: the fraction of bags you get back. Fidelity: whether they come back undamaged and unopened. Storage time: how long before bags start going missing. Bandwidth: how big a bag fits through the door. Multimode capacity: how many lockers there are.

Where it failsA luggage office can photocopy your documents; a quantum memory cannot copy an unknown state (no-cloning). A “lost bag” in a memory can also become noise added to other bags.

Worked example · how many pulses fit?

With ODEIT ≈ 9.6 the time–bandwidth product is √9.6 ≈ 3. Only about three distinguishable pulses fit in the medium at once. Raising it to 30 gives ≈ 5.5. This is why OD is the resource (paper Sec. IV). The exponential storage-decay form above is a simple model; real decay curves can be non-exponential (e.g. diffusion).

Q1. Can a memory have 99 % fidelity and 10 % efficiency?
Yes. Fidelity (conditional on retrieval) and efficiency are separate figures of merit. Many demonstrations report both.
Q2. Why does the project first benchmark with classical (attenuated) light?
Efficiency, delay, bandwidth and storage time can be measured with weak classical pulses on standard photodiodes. That calibrates the platform before single-photon sources and detectors (L11, Objective 3) are introduced.

L11

Quantum states of light: coherent, Fock, single photons, g⁽²⁾(0)

Learning goals
  • Contrast coherent states (lasers) with Fock states (single photons).
  • Use Poisson statistics to analyse attenuated laser pulses.
  • Interpret the second-order correlation g⁽²⁾(0).

A laser, however strongly attenuated, emits a coherent state: the photon number in a pulse is Poisson-distributed. Attenuation lowers the mean but never removes the chance of two photons. A true single-photon source (e.g. the α-MoO₃ emitters planned for Objective 3) ideally emits a Fock state |1⟩ with zero chance of two. The Hanbury Brown–Twiss measurement of g⁽²⁾(0) tells them apart.

L11.1\[ P(n)=e^{-\bar n}\frac{\bar n^{\,n}}{n!},\qquad g^{(2)}(0)=\frac{\langle\hat a^\dagger\hat a^\dagger\hat a\hat a\rangle}{\langle\hat a^\dagger\hat a\rangle^2}=\begin{cases}1&\text{coherent}\\ 2&\text{thermal}\\ 0&\text{single-photon Fock state}\end{cases} \]
Rain on a tile versus a vending machine

Read it in wordsFor a coherent state, the probability of n photons follows a Poisson law with mean n̄. g⁽²⁾(0) compares the chance of detecting two photons at the same time with what independent arrivals would give: 1 for a laser, 2 for thermal (bunched) light, 0 for an ideal single-photon source.

AnalogyRaindrops hitting one floor tile arrive randomly: sometimes none, occasionally two at once (Poisson, g⁽²⁾ = 1). A vending machine drops exactly one can per button press, never two (Fock state, g⁽²⁾ = 0).

Where it failsRaindrops are classical particles. Light’s statistics come from the quantum state of a field mode, and a single photon can still be split between two paths without being divided.

Worked example · attenuated probe pulses

A 10 µs, 1 µW pulse at 795 nm holds ≈ 4.0 × 10⁷ photons (L1). For n̄ = 1, attenuate by 4 × 10⁷ (ND ≈ 7.6). At n̄ = 0.1: P(0) = 0.905, P(1) = 0.090, P(≥2) = 0.0047. About 5 % of non-empty pulses still contain two or more photons, which is why “single-photon-level” classical probes are not single photons.

Q1. What g⁽²⁾(0) would you measure for the Method A probe laser?
≈ 1 (coherent light), regardless of attenuation.
Q2. Why does storing true single photons demand much better control filtering than storing a µW probe?
The signal is ~1 photon per pulse, while a mW control leaks, scatters and fluoresces. Even a 10⁻⁵ extinction (Glan–Taylor) leaves many control photons per pulse. Spectral filtering and timing gates are then needed too.

L12

Strong coupling, cavity QED and polaritons in solids: the bridge to Objective 3

Learning goals
  • Define the strong-coupling condition and cooperativity.
  • See how an ensemble gives collective enhancement g√N.
  • Understand, without overclaiming, how microcavity polaritons relate to this project.

Put an emitter in an optical cavity. If the emitter–photon coupling g beats both the cavity loss κ and the emitter decay γ, energy oscillates back and forth and the spectrum splits into two polariton peaks separated by the vacuum Rabi splitting. N emitters coupled to the same mode act collectively with g√N. The EIT paper makes the same point for vapours: strong light–matter coupling arises from collective enhancement of a large ensemble, and “no high-quality cavity is required”.

Objective 3 concerns solid-state systems. Literature cited in the project plan reports ultrastrong coupling in regioregular P3HT on plasmonic arrays (Rabi splitting ~0.6 eV) and hyperbolic phonon polaritons in α-MoO₃. Whether such systems can support EIT-like storage at room temperature is precisely the open question the calibrated Rb platform is meant to help test. Nothing here should be read as a claim that they do.

L12.1\[ g\gg\kappa,\gamma\ \ (\text{strong coupling}),\qquad C=\frac{4g^2}{\kappa\gamma},\qquad \hbar\Omega_R=2\hbar g\sqrt{N}\ \ (\text{collective vacuum Rabi splitting}),\qquad \frac{g}{\omega}\gtrsim0.1\ \ (\text{a common ultrastrong criterion}). \]
Two pendulums joined by a spring

Read it in wordsStrong coupling means the exchange rate g outruns both loss channels. Cooperativity C summarises how good the coupling is. N emitters together couple √N times more strongly. “Ultrastrong” is a regime where g becomes a sizeable fraction of the bare frequency itself; ~0.1 is a commonly used threshold.

AnalogyTwo pendulums joined by a spring trade their swing back and forth. Strongly joined, they no longer swing at their own frequencies but in two new shared modes, one faster and one slower. That frequency split is the Rabi splitting, and the shared modes are the polaritons.

Where it failsPendulums are classical. Vacuum Rabi splitting exists even with zero photons in the cavity, and polariton storage needs long-lived matter coherence. A large splitting alone does not guarantee that.

Worked example · same idea, different scales

In the Rb cell, the “coupling” that matters is collective: OD ≈ 10 means the ensemble absorbs strongly without any cavity. In the P3HT work cited in the project plan the splitting is quoted at ~0.6 eV project plan lit., compared with 1.56 eV for a 795 nm photon (L1). These are very different physical regimes, which is why Objective 3 begins with careful calibration on the Rb platform.

Q1. Why does an atomic ensemble not need a cavity to reach strong collective coupling?
The relevant figure, OD, grows with atom number. Many atoms each weakly coupled add up (g√N, or OD ∝ N L), so a single pass through a dense cell gives strong light–matter interaction.
Q2. Does a large Rabi splitting imply a good quantum memory?
No. A memory also needs a long-lived, controllable coherence (like γ₁₂ in Rb) and a way to switch the coupling on and off. Splitting measures coupling strength, not storage time.
