      Quantum Spain-style bench: what the photograph shows

      This bench is reconstructed from a single photograph of a Quantum Spain-style EIT bench, not from drawings. Positions are approximate, part names marked “look” or “class” are look-alikes, and anything not visible is marked “assumed”. The optical chain is this guide’s own Zeeman-EIT chain (same physics as the 3D Bench tab) re-laid on the two boards; in the simulator the lock, the EIT window and the pulse storage all work.

      Reading of the bench

      • Left breadboard: laser input and SAS lock. Fibre-coupled laser light (blue PM patch cables with green FC/APC connectors), isolator, steering mirrors, a pick-off, and the saturated-absorption arm with the white 3D-printed reference-cell module (banana jacks and a small board on top).
      • Right breadboard (Thorlabs MB4545/M): the EIT board. Two red-housed AOMs with gold SMA inputs, many waveplates, cubes and lenses, the white EIT-cell module with red heater and coil leads, and a black detector or camera housing at the right edge.
      • Shelf behind the boards: two Thorlabs benchtop controllers, two Rigol generators, two TENMA DC supplies labelled HEATER and COILS, and a Rigol oscilloscope at each end of the boards.

      Assumption about the lock. The two double-pass AOMs shift the beams by +160 MHz, and the SAS arm has no AOM. The simulator treats the lock point as the atomic resonance, which hides that offset. A real bench needs the lock moved by about 160 MHz (for example with an AOM in the SAS arm), and the photograph does not show how this is done.

      What this bench can do

      • Laser lock to the Rb D1 line. The SAS arm and the reference cell on board 1, with the Thorlabs current and TEC controllers (about 147 mA is a normal 795 nm DFB drive current) and a Rigol generator for the dither, read on a scope.
      • Continuous-wave Zeeman EIT. Control and probe from two AOM arms, opposite circular polarizations, an EIT cell with a heater (about 1.2 W DC) and a solenoid (about 80 mA); linewidth versus control intensity, magnetic field and temperature.
      • Slow light and light storage. The two-channel Rigol generator can drive the probe pulse and switch the control AOM off and on, with the second scope on the probe and the trigger.
      • Field sensing. The Zeeman EIT line moves 1.4 kHz per milligauss, so the bench can double as a magnetometer.
      • Parts the guide’s list does not have: the cat’s-eye lenses in the two AOM arms need a focal length of about 75 mm (80 mm after the AOM, 70 mm to the retro mirror); the control beam expander is f = 30 mm plus f = 100 mm, 130 mm apart.
      • Not possible with what is visible: hyperfine (6.8 GHz) EIT (no microwave source or EOM), single-photon-level detection (no photon counters), and absolute efficiency measurements (no calibrated detection).

      Why it differs from our bill of materials

      • Fewer mounts: 39 post sets and 9 mirrors in the Spain-style version, against 60 post sets and 11 mirrors in ours.
      • The cell is heated from a plain DC supply instead of a temperature controller.
      • No optical power meter, no multimeter, no fluxgate magnetometer and no laser-safety items.

      Caveats

      • The real photo shows more mounts than the simulated layout (about 20–25 mirrors against 9); each extra mirror with its mount and post adds one more line of cost.
      • The laser is priced as this guide’s DFB (the laser in the photo is not visible), and the two-channel Rigol source is priced as this guide’s two-channel RF synthesiser.
      • Prices are the guide’s planning prices, not quotations.
