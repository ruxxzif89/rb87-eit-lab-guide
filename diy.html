What this tab is, how to read the drawings, safety, and the order of work

Ten parts of the Zeeman-EIT bench are made in the lab and not bought as finished instruments: the solenoid, the cell stack, the ITC502 cables, the degaussing coil, a backup current source, the lock-box divider, the fluxgate supply, the RF control box, the AOM platform, and the beam dumps and covers. Each one below has a purpose, a parts list (no prices), tools, dimensioned drawings and wiring schematics, numbered build steps with a check after every step, tests, the usual mistakes, and the items you must still measure or confirm before you cut or solder.

How to read the drawings

Units and scale

Lengths are in millimetres unless a unit is written. The subtitle of each drawing gives its scale (for example “5 px per mm”). Side views of the former and the cell stack are drawn to scale; schematics are not.

Colours

Red: beam, positive supply, heat. Blue: AOM, negative supply, return. Green: reference, thermistor, OK. Orange: feedback, measurement, micrometer. Hatching: cut plastic or metal.

Open and print

Click a drawing to open the full-size SVG in a new tab, then zoom with Ctrl + scroll. On a phone, slide the drawing sideways or tap it. The page prints with each drawing at full width (Ctrl + P), one part per sheet.

Safety for every part

Laser: 795 nm, invisible

The DFB label says Class 4. Wear goggles rated for 795 nm, keep every beam in a dump or a tube (D10), and never look along a beam or at a reflection. Build and test the electronics (D3 to D8) with the laser off.

Mains (only D4)

The degaussing drive uses a variac on the mains. Build it in a closed, earthed metal box with fuses and cable clamps, and only if you are competent to do mains wiring. The alternative with a function generator needs no mains work.

Magnetism

No steel, nickel-plated or magnetic part inside or near the shield: screws, tweezers, connector shells, clips, soldering-iron stands. Test suspect parts with a small compass. Keep magnets, magnetic screwdrivers and phones away from the shield.

Heat

The cell stack runs at 65 °C and the D4 series resistor dissipates up to 19 W. Put the resistor on a heatsink, away from the shield, and let both cool before you touch them.

RF

The amplifiers give about 1 W (+30 dBm). Never run an amplifier with its output open or shorted, keep SMA connectors tight, and set the attenuator to 31.5 dB before the first power-up.

Solenoid former and winding

The 50 mG bias field along the beams, inside the shield

BOM C07about 8 h + machiningneeds the shield bore

The solenoid makes the uniform magnetic field along the beams that splits the Zeeman levels: 50 mG gives a two-photon splitting of 70 kHz (1.4 kHz per mG). The EIT line is only about 7 kHz wide, so the field must be the same everywhere in the cell to a fraction of 0.2 % (0.1 mG, a 140 Hz shift). The design below was computed ring by ring with the exact Biot–Savart formula (checked against a brute-force sum to 10⁻¹²) and gives 0.20 % over the cell.

What the design does

  • One helical groove in a plastic tube holds the wire exactly: 36 turns at 3.6 mm pitch in the middle and 3 turns at 1.8 mm pitch at each end. The closer end turns make up for the field that falls off at the ends of a finite coil: the error over the cell drops from 0.42 % to 0.20 %.
  • Four end turns would give more margin. The design just meets the 0.2 % target at the very ends of the 25 mm path (about half of it on average). Four turns at 1.8 mm at each end (a groove 144 mm long, 44 turns) give 0.15 %: use them if you cannot centre the cell to better than 1 mm.
  • Two return wires 180° apart run back along the axis, each carrying half the current. A helix carries a net axial current; a single return wire would add a transverse field of 0.10 mG per mA at the axis, and two wires at 180° cancel it.
  • Plastic only (PEEK, Delrin or printed PETG): any steel or nickel near the shield magnetises it and shifts the field by amounts far larger than 0.1 mG.

Build steps

  1. Get the shield data. Ask the maker, or read the drawing, for the innermost bore, the inner length and the end-cap feed-through. The former needs a bore of at least Ø46 mm (the former plus 3.45 mm of radial clearance), an inner length of at least 250 mm, and the winding then stays about 55 mm from each end cap. A smaller bore needs a thinner former and a new field calculation, because the winding radius enters directly.Check You have the bore and the length in writing.
  2. Make the former. Follow the former drawing. Cut the groove as one right-hand helix, 0.60 wide and 0.40 deep, rounded at the edges (a sharp edge cuts the enamel). Scribe a light ring at the mid-plane and mark the cable end (the end where the leads leave).Check wall 2.0 ± 0.1 mm; ten pitches measure 36.0 ± 0.2 mm; the groove starts and ends 70.2 mm from the mid-plane (140.4 mm long); no burrs.
  3. Prepare the wire and the rig. Cut off 5.9 m for the helix with its start lead (0.8 m of lead + 4.98 m of helix + a little spare) and keep the rest of the reel for the two return wires (0.2 m each) and the finish lead (0.8 m). Put the spool on a friction brake so that the wire runs under 50 to 100 g of pull. Handle it only with plastic or brass tools.Check the wire runs without kinks and the enamel is not scraped.
  4. Wind the helix. Start at the cable end (z = −70.2 mm). Lay 3 turns at 1.8 mm pitch, then 36 turns at 3.6 mm, then 3 turns at 1.8 mm; the finish is at z = +70.2 mm. Count aloud and mark every tenth turn with a dot of marker on the former.Check exactly 42 turns. The resistance of the helix alone is about 0.44 Ω (4.98 m at 0.0876 Ω/m).
  5. Fix the end turns. Put a drop of cyanoacrylate or epoxy on the first and last 3 turns and on every tenth turn. Do not glue the other turns: glue stress on the wire shifts it in the groove.Check no turn can move when you push it with a plastic tool.
  6. Lay the two return wires. At the far end, scrape the enamel from the helix end and from one end of each return wire, twist the three together and solder (one joint). Lay the returns along the axis at 90° and 270° (see the winding plan), tape them every 40 mm with polyimide tape, and at the cable end solder them together into the finish lead.Check resistance from start lead to finish lead: 0.58 Ω ± 0.03 (the two returns in parallel add only 0.007 Ω).
  7. Twist the leads. Twist the start and finish leads together, about one twist per 20 mm, and solder the plug of D3 (LD OUT). Mark the start lead red.Check no loop of area in the leads: they run as one twisted pair.
  8. Check the field outside the shield. Put the former on a plastic stand a metre away from steel, with the fluxgate (D7) at the centre. Apply +10.00 mA, record the reading, apply −10.00 mA and record again. The difference removes the Earth field (about 0.4 G) and the sensor offset.Check B(+10 mA) − B(−10 mA) = 67.8 mG ± 5 % (3.39 mG/mA × 20 mA).
  9. Mark the field direction. With the same measurement, note which current polarity gives a field along the beam direction (z, away from the laser) and engrave or label “B+” on the former.Check the label agrees with the fluxgate sign.
  10. Install and calibrate in the shield. Push the former into the innermost layer with the cell stack (D2) inside, centre it with the two spacer rings and close the end caps with the leads through the feed-through. After the degauss (D4), put the fluxgate plate (D7) in place of the cell and measure B against I at ±5, ±10 and ±15 mA.Check a straight line B = k·I; use the measured k (a few per cent above 3.39 mG/mA, because the shield pulls the flux in) in every later experiment, not the formula. The fluxgate scale is good to ± 2 %: confirm k with the atoms (the EIT line moves 1.4 kHz per mG).

Mistakes to avoid

  • Steel tools, steel screws or nickel-plated connector shells near the former or the shield: they magnetise and the field no longer follows the formula.
  • A helix without the two return wires, or with only one: the transverse field is 0.10 mG per mA, 1.5 mG at 14.7 mA.
  • Winding under too much pull: the groove edge cuts the enamel and two turns short together.
  • Different pitch at the ends: the error over the cell rises from 0.20 % to 0.42 %.
  • Loose, untwisted leads that move: the loop area changes and the field at the cell follows.
Confirm before you start
  • Innermost shield bore ≥ Ø46 mm, inner length ≥ 250 mm and an end-cap feed-through of Ø20 mm. The BOM line “Ø~70 × 250 mm, 3 layers” gives only the overall size: three layers and two gaps would share the 12 mm of radius between Ø46 and Ø70 (gaps of about 4 mm).
  • Cell dimensions and the stem from the vendor drawing (D2).
  • Who cuts the groove and with which pitch tolerance: the drawing needs ± 0.05 mm per pitch.

Cell stack: cell, heater, thermistor, foam and tubes

Holds the cell on the axis at 55 to 65 °C with no magnetic field of its own

BOM C01 to C04, M04about 8 hneeds the cell drawing

The cell sits on the axis in the middle of the solenoid, warm enough for an optical depth near 10 (55 to 65 °C), with a heater that makes no magnetic field, a temperature sensor the ITC502 can read, and enough insulation to hold the temperature with about 2 W. There is no metal near the cell. The heater is built as a bifilar pair (go and return side by side) so that its field cancels; a purchased foil is allowed only if its layout is paired and the test below passes.

What the design does

  • NTC and not Pt100. The ITC502 resolves 1 Ω on its thermistor range: a Pt100 gives 2.6 K per ohm, a 10 kΩ NTC gives 14 mK per ohm at 65 °C.
  • Foam sleeve and two Delrin tubes hold the temperature with 0.9 to 2.1 W and keep the whole stack on the axis: tube 63 + sleeve 34 + tube 63 = 160 mm, the length of the former.
  • Diode in series with the heater. A resistor cannot cool: when the controller asks for cooling it would reverse the current, which heats again and runs away. The diode lets only one polarity through; its orientation is found with the dummy-load test of D3.
  • Unpaired heater wire is a coil. One unpaired turn of 0.54 A at the end of the heater gives 97 mG at the cell centre, 2000 times what we can accept. That is why the pattern is paired.

Mistakes to avoid

  • A heater wound as one coil, or a foil with a return bus along one edge: that is an unpaired turn (97 mG at the cell from 0.54 A). Always run the on-and-off test.
  • A Pt100 on the ITC502: 1 Ω is 2.6 K, so the temperature would step by 2.6 K.
  • No diode: a resistor heater runs away when the controller tries to cool.
  • A cold window: Rb condenses on the coldest glass. The coldest point should be the stem, not a window; ask for a stem and keep the windows inside the foam sleeve ends.
  • Thermistor leads running through the heater field region as a loop: twist them and keep them flat.
Confirm before you start
  • The real cell: diameter, length, window thickness, stem position and length. The drawing assumes Ø25 × 28 mm with no stem; a stem needs a 10 × 14 mm slot in the sleeve and the former.
  • Foam and epoxy: the temperature rating and the absence of iron or nickel in the data sheets.
  • A bought foil heater: ask for the trace layout and accept only a paired or symmetric pattern, then run the on-and-off test.

What the design does

  • Heater: TEC pins 5, 6, 7 are tied together as “+”, pins 13, 14, 15 as “−” (the manual says always use all three pins), the thermistor on pins 4 and 3. The diode is in the heater + wire.

What the design does

  • Toroidal winding. The field in the wall of a layer at radius r is H = N·√2·I / (2π r): 193 A/m peak at 1 A rms in the outer layer. Mu-metal saturates at a few tens of A/m, so 1 A rms is already well above saturation in every layer, and the procedure uses up to 2 A rms.

Mistakes to avoid

  • An open or shorted amplifier output: the data sheet says an open load can damage it and derates the maximum input by 20 dB. Always have the AOM or a 50 Ω load connected.
  • Powering the amplifier before the bias box, or the synthesizer before the amplifier: use the order bias box, +24 V, synthesizer; and the reverse to switch off.
  • The synthesizer above 0 dBm: 0 dBm already gives about +30 dBm out, and the amplifier takes at most +10 dBm in.
  • DC on an RF line: the switch and the attenuator both require the RF ports to be held at 0 V or DC-blocked.
  • Overheating the DC pins of the attenuator when soldering; or soldering the wrong rail to the wrong pin (P2 Vdd +3 V, P4 Vss −3 V).
  • Mixing the two arms: label them, and keep the 35 kHz difference between 80.000 and 79.965 MHz in the synthesizer, not in the amplifiers.

AOM yaw platform and the Bragg set-up

A micrometer on an 80 mm lever sets the Bragg angle to 0.1 mrad; then the double pass is aligned

BOM M04, O17, O08about 8 hneeds the AOM drawing

An AOM diffracts well only when the beam meets the sound fronts at the Bragg angle θB = λf / 2v: 7.57 mrad (0.43°) at 795 nm and 80 MHz, with v = 4.2 mm/µs (Gooch & Housego data for the 3080 family). The acceptance of a 1.5 mm beam is only about 0.5 mrad, so the AOM needs an angular adjustment of about 0.1 mrad about the vertical axis. A micrometer pushing a tail 80 mm from the pivot gives 0.125 mrad per 10 µm division and ±81 mrad of range. After the platform, the double pass is aligned: a quarter-wave plate, the f = 100 mm lens as a cat’s eye, a retro mirror, a razor blade that stops the 0th order, and the polarizing beamsplitter that picks the returned beam.

What the design does

  • Lever and micrometer. A carrier plate turns on a brass pivot; the micrometer spindle pushes its tail and a spring plunger pushes back, so there is no play. One division (10 µm) at 80 mm is 0.125 mrad, about a fifth of the Bragg angle, so the maximum can be found by hand.
  • Cat’s eye. The lens puts the retro mirror at its focus, so a change of the RF frequency (which changes the deflection angle) does not walk the returning beam. With f = 100 mm the two orders are 1.5 mm apart at the mirror; a razor-blade edge placed between them on a small stage stops the zeroth order.
  • Polarization. The quarter-wave plate is crossed twice, so the returned beam is turned by 90° and the polarizing beamsplitter sends it out of the arm.

Calculations

  • Solenoid field and uniformity: exact ring-by-ring Biot–Savart sum (elliptic integrals), checked against a brute-force sum to 10⁻¹²; the volume is the cell, ρ ≤ 12.5 mm and |z| ≤ 12.5 mm; the shield is not modelled (it raises the field by a few per cent): calibrate in place.
  • Heater and thermal numbers: a lumped estimate with foam k = 0.05 W/m/K, h = 8 W/m²K and a heat capacity of 8 J/K, once for the stack in free air (upper bound of the loss) and once as a series chain through the former, the shield gaps and the outside. They are estimates: measure your own stack.
  • RF levels: the arithmetic of the data-sheet values (typical gain 33 dB, insertion loss 1.3 dB and 1.5 dB); the coupler read-out is Vpp = √(8 · 50 Ω · 10(P − 19.5)/10 / 1000).
  • AOM numbers: θB = λf / 2v with v = 4.2 mm/µs; the saturation power at 795 nm scales with λ² from the 633 nm value (an estimate).
  • All numbers in the text and in the drawings come from the same set of parameters, so they agree with each other.
